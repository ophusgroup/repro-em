# Objections and exceptions

Every argument on this site has a reasonable counterargument, and most of them are raised by people acting in good faith, so they are worth answering directly.

## Proprietary and embargoed data

Industrial collaborations, export-controlled materials, and pre-patent work all come with genuine restrictions, and no framework that ignores them will be adopted.

The useful distinction is between data that cannot be released now and data that will never be released. An embargo with a fixed expiry date, a registered DOI, and a deposited record is compatible with everything on this site, while an indefinite restriction with no expiry is not.

Even when the data itself is restricted, the metadata schema, the calibration methods, the analysis code, and the pipeline can almost always be published. A reader who can inspect the method but not the specimen is in a far better position than one who has neither.

If nothing can be shared, say so explicitly in the paper, and say why. A reader who knows a claim rests on unavailable data can weight it accordingly, which makes undeclared restriction the more damaging case.

## Curation effort and who pays for it

This objection is a fair one. Preparing a dataset for deposition properly takes hours to days, that time is unfunded, and it falls on the graduate student who is already late on a thesis chapter.

Most of that cost exists because curation is done at the end, by hand, reconstructing facts the instrument knew at acquisition time and discarded. Metadata written by the microscope at the moment of recording costs nothing. Metadata reconstructed from a lab notebook eighteen months later costs days and is often wrong. The expense is a symptom of the problem this framework is trying to fix, so it should not be used as an argument for keeping it.

The rest is a funding question. Data management plans are already required by most agencies and are neither funded nor enforced. Central facilities are well placed to do this work once for many users, and are rarely resourced to do it. See [calls to action](calls-to-action.md).

## Scooping and priority

The fear is that a competitor downloads your data, reprocesses it with a better method, and publishes before you do.

Embargo periods address the acute version of this, and a dataset DOI issued at deposition establishes priority in a citable, timestamped form that a private hard drive never can. Structural biology ran this experiment on itself decades ago, and mandatory deposition did not destroy anyone's career.

Underlying this is a question of credit, since reprocessing feels like theft partly because datasets are not counted as research products in the way that papers are. If a dataset were citable, indexed, and creditable on a CV, then sharing it would become an investment rather than a loss.

## When the framework does not apply

The evidentiary standard should scale with the strength of the claim being made.

Instrument alignment, routine quality control, exploratory sessions, teaching data, and failed experiments do not need deposition, validation reports, or chains of custody, and neither does a survey micrograph illustrating a qualitative statement that no one disputes.

The full treatment belongs to any measurement that a published conclusion actually rests on: a quantitative number with an uncertainty, a structure determination, a reconstruction, or a claim that some feature exists. A reasonable test is whether a reader would otherwise have to take our word for it.
