# Chain of custody

Chain of custody is a legal idea, borrowed here without much modification. Evidence is admissible when every transfer from the scene to the courtroom is documented, and when any step in that record is missing, the evidence is not trusted no matter how compelling it looks.

Our situation is the same. A published figure is an assertion about a physical sample, made after a chain of transformations that the reader cannot see. The claim is only as strong as the weakest undocumented link.

## From detector to figure

The chain has a small number of links, and each one needs the same three things: an identified input, a recorded transformation, and an identified output.

1. **Detector output**, signed at the instrument where possible. See [validated detectors](detectors.md).
2. **Raw file**, immutable, with a content hash and a persistent identifier.
3. **Calibration and correction**, recorded as products that reference the raw rather than replacing it.
4. **Reconstruction or reduction**, with its code version, parameters, and seeds. See [computational imaging](computational-imaging.md).
5. **Analysis**, likewise. See [analysis](analysis.md).
6. **Figure**, generated by a script from the archived product. See [plotting](plotting.md).

Content hashing is what makes the chain checkable rather than merely stated. Identify every artifact by the hash of its contents, and record the hashes of the inputs to every step. A reader can then verify that the file they downloaded is the file the analysis actually ran on, which no amount of prose in a methods section can establish.

None of this requires new technology. Version control, content hashing, and containers are ordinary infrastructure everywhere except in our labs.

## Authenticity in the age of generative AI

We simulate electron scattering extremely well, which has always been a strength of this field and is now also an exposure. A convincing HAADF image of a material that does not exist is within reach of anyone with a laptop, and the same is true of a diffraction pattern or a spectrum ([Davydiuk et al., *Nat. Nanotechnol.* 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41565-025-02009-9)).

Detection is the wrong response. Any detector for synthetic images becomes a training signal for the next generator, and that contest does not end in our favor. The durable answer is provenance: rather than asking whether an image looks real, ask whether it can be traced to a signed detector output through a documented chain.

This inverts the burden in a useful way. Fabricating an image gets easier every year. Fabricating a complete, internally consistent, cryptographically signed chain of custody does not.

The same infrastructure catches ordinary error, which is far more common than fraud and does more cumulative damage. A pipeline that has to declare its inputs and parameters is a pipeline where a wrong calibration or a transposed axis has somewhere to be caught.

## Independent verification

The chain is only worth building if someone can walk it. That means a reader should be able to download the raw data, run the published pipeline in the published environment, and obtain the published figure.

Two things make this realistic rather than aspirational. The pipeline has to run without access to any resource the authors have and the reader does not, which rules out hard-coded local paths, private data, and unpinned dependencies. And it has to be tested by someone other than its author before publication, because a pipeline that has only ever run on the machine that wrote it is not yet reproducible.

This is a job journals could enforce, and a job funding agencies could pay for. See [calls to action](../calls-to-action.md).
