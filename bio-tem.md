# Learning from bio TEM

The biological electron microscopy community has been enormously successful at defining shared deposition and validation standards through open-access archives.

For structural biology, the PDB and the EMDB provide stable identifiers, community metadata expectations, and established validation pathways that make results reusable and comparable across laboratories ([Rose et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkw1000); [wwPDB Consortium, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkad1019)). For cryo-electron tomography, the same philosophy extends upstream to raw and intermediate data, with EMPIAR and the CryoET Data Portal enabling broad reprocessing, method development, and independent verification on shared datasets ([Iudin et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkac1062); [Ermel et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02477-2)).

These resources normalized the idea that preservation and validation are essential parts of characterization science.

## What made it work

The archives are the visible part of the system, and three further mechanisms are what hold it together.

Deposition became a condition of publication, so that journals will not print a structure without an accession code. This is enforced by editors rather than by the goodwill of authors, and it is what gives the remaining mechanisms any practical effect.

Validation is automatic and public. A deposited structure receives a validation report generated by the archive rather than by the authors, and that report is distributed with the entry, so a reviewer can read it instead of relying on a methods section.

The community also agreed on a single number. Resolution measured by FSC, computed the same way for every entry, means that any two results in the archive can be compared directly. See [benchmarks](framework/benchmarks.md).

## What does not transfer

Biological EM converges on a small number of questions with a shared answer format, and that is what makes a single deposition schema and a single validation metric workable. Materials TEM does not have this, and previous standardization efforts have generally failed by assuming otherwise. See [materials science TEM](materials-tem.md).

The parts that do transfer are structural rather than technical: deposition tied to publication, validation computed by someone other than the author, and metrics that the community agrees to report. Each of these can be applied without assuming that everyone is performing the same experiment.
