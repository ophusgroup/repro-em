# Academia

Academic groups produce most of the materials TEM data that reaches the literature, and they operate under conditions that work against reproducibility at almost every point.

Data is usually recorded by a graduate student or postdoc, analyzed with scripts written for that project, and stored on a lab drive under a naming convention known to one person. When that person leaves, the practical ability to reproduce the work leaves with them. Availability decays for exactly this reason, and it decays fast. Across 516 papers spanning two decades, the odds of recovering a usable dataset fell about 17% for every year since publication ([Vines et al., *Curr. Biol.* 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2013.11.014)).

The incentives point the same way. Curation is unfunded and uncredited, papers are the unit of career progress, and datasets are not counted as research products. A student who spends a week preparing a deposition has spent a week not writing.

Two things would change this without changing anyone's values. Metadata captured automatically at acquisition removes most of the curation cost, because the expensive part is reconstructing facts the instrument already knew. And making datasets citable and creditable turns deposition into something that advances a career rather than delaying one. See [objections and exceptions](../objections.md).

Academic groups also write most of the open source analysis software the field depends on, which is the part of this landscape that already works well.
